Google treated Google Earth like a canvas. Journalists, human-rights investigators, open-source researchers, and courts treated it like a witness. When Google added native generative editing, then rolled it back a day later, that mismatch became visible.

Ordinary consumer software helps people create. Public evidence infrastructure helps institutions establish what happened.

Adding generation to the second category is not a feature launch. It is a change in institutional role: the same system now records reality and manufactures plausible alternatives to it.

Google appears to have evaluated the intended use, downstream guardrails, and watermarking. But images leave the product through screenshots, crops, and reposts. The verification burden then lands on the people who relied on the platform’s provenance.

Once a product becomes evidence infrastructure, the communities that depend on it need standing in pre-deployment review. Classify the social role before approving the roadmap—not after the rollback.
