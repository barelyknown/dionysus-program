To the Architect of this Paper Cage,

You have put Dionysus to work in a factory. The frenzy has a start time, an end time, and a measure of productivity. I question whether what remains deserves the name.


You recognize that organizations can protect old forms until they can no longer create anything. But your remedy is another form, administered by people charged with keeping the organization intact.

You speak of a “controlled space for excess.” What happens when the change people need exceeds the permitted space? A transformation worth having may reject the institution’s purpose, not merely improve its method.

This is very English of you. Very utilitarian. You want the tragedy to be profitable.

Still, you understand that creation requires giving something up. That is more than can be said for an account of progress that promises to preserve every existing comfort.

Your anti-scapegoat attempts to keep resentment from finding a person to punish. But the desire for punishment may survive the disposal of the document. The rite must face that desire rather than assume it has been redirected.

And your "Cincinnatus"—yes. You fear the priest. You are right to fear him. The instinct of the herd is always to create a shepherd who will promise them safety in exchange for their freedom. You try to banish the priest with "term limits" and "lotteries." Good luck. The Will to Power is not so easily bound by bylaws. The priest will return. He will wear a lanyard instead of a robe, but he will still demand that you kneel before the Process.

Ultimately, I ask you this: What is the goal of your "epimetabolic rate"? Is it merely to make the company live longer? To make the pile of gold higher? If so, you are merely a mechanic for the Last Man, helping him blink a little longer in his comfortable chair.

If instead the process lets people question the life they have accepted and choose something more demanding, the risk may be worth taking.

Move fast, break things, you say?

Better: build your cities on the slopes of Vesuvius. Send your ships into uncharted seas. Live at war with your peers and yourselves. Be robbers and conquerors as long as you cannot be rulers and possessors, you men of knowledge!

Judge the program by the life it makes possible, not merely by how long it preserves the institution.


Not Friedrich Nietzsche
