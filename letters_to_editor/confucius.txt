To the Author of the Dionysus Program,

Your distinction between ritual form and humane conduct is the part of the program I would put first.

You cite me in your text. You speak of the distinction I drew between the form of the cup and the water that fills it. This is well seen. When the heart is absent, the bow is just a bending of the back; when trust is absent, the meeting is just a noise of mouths. You call this the Readiness Gate. I called it the root. If the root is neglected, what grows will be a weed.

I lived in a time when the world was melting. The music was corrupt, the names were wrong, and sons did not know how to be sons. My solution was to look backward, to the Duke of Zhou, to restore the old patterns. You look forward, to the fire and the breaking. This frightens the timid. But I see what you are doing. You are trying to build a vessel that can hold fire without cracking.

Your Epimetabolic Rate is a strange phrase for a simple truth: the gentleman is not he who makes no mistakes, but he who corrects them quickly. To have faults and not to reform them—this, indeed, should be called having faults. You have built a machine to make the reformation of faults a public ceremony. You treat the error as an offering, not a crime. This is a way to make the people brave.

The Roman farmer’s return to ordinary work gives authority a limit. The same limit should apply to those who conduct your rites.

A warning: intensity does not establish that a ritual is working. Judge it by the conduct of the people afterward, including their treatment of those who disagreed.

You write that you wish to move fast by breaking things. I would say: move only as fast as you can mend. But perhaps in your age, the ground moves so fast that standing still is the only way to break.


The forms may be new. The obligation to practice what they require remains.


Signed,

Not Confucius
